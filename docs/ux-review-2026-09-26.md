# Vote Room UX review, 26 September 2026

This is a heuristic review and a **simulated walkthrough**, not a usability test with voters. It reviews the mobile path first (360 px portrait), then desktop. The fifty checks below are an applied checklist synthesized from the sources, not a published “top 50” ranking. A check marked partial remains a real limitation.

## Three flow designs

| Design | Simulated path to a party page, starting as a guest | What works | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Value first (chosen)** | Welcome → guest → one orientation tap (or show all) → party page. Three actions. | Direct comparison; short clip, two arguments and personal follow link near the top; preference depth is optional. Returning visitors resume their saved choices. | Initially no personal grade; it appears after the visitor has rated two topics. |
| B. Guided questionnaire | Welcome → guest → orientation → choose topics → continue → party page. Around five actions. | More tailored initial cards. | Delays the first useful content; prompts become a second gate on a phone. |
| C. Video first | Welcome → guest → orientation → featured clip → party page or next clip. At least three actions, plus watching. | Human connection quickly. | Hard to scan fourteen lists; videos are slow or unavailable for some visitors. |

Simulated mobile cases: a distracted first visitor in A can reach a party page in three taps and see arguments before choosing any preference; a visitor unsure about Netanyahu can choose “show all” and compare lists; a returning visitor can open `/map` directly and see their saved shortlist. In B, the first visitor must answer another question before seeing any list. In C, comparing several parties requires watching or skipping each clip. These are task walkthroughs, **not measured completion times**.

## Why this design

Progressive disclosure keeps the main task visible while preserving depth. A single orientation choice is a useful starting point, but the visitor can see all lists without answering. The optional coalition questions are presented honestly as saved reflections: they do not currently drive matching. A one-topic 100/100 score was especially misleading, so the personal score now requires at least two visitor-rated topics and shows its coverage.

Primary research and standards used: [NN/g progressive disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/), [ten usability heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/), [recognition rather than recall](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/), [information scent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/), [concise/scannable copy](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/), [optional registration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/optional-registration/), [GOV.UK question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/), [GOV.UK details](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/details/), [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag/), and [W3C touch target guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum). These inform a heuristic review; they do not prove this particular site is usable without observation of real visitors.

## Next user study

Ask five participants with different voting positions to use their own phones: find a party they do not know, play a short clip, identify the strongest objection, mark their impression, and return later to change it. Observe where they hesitate and whether they mistake their own score for a recommendation. Do not collect their political choices in a research recording without informed consent.
